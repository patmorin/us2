Reviewer E:

An l-vertex ranking of a graph G is a coloring of the vertices of G with integer colors so that in any connected subgraph H of G with diameter at most l there is a vertex in H whose color is larger than any other vertex in H. The authors study l-vertex rankings of degenerate graphs using the minimum possible number of colors. Given a graph G let chi-l(G) denote the minimum number of colors required for a coloring of G that is an l-vertex ranking.

The main result of this paper is the following. Assuming that d and l are constants, for every n-vertex d-degenerate graph G, we have chi-l(G) in O(n^(1 - 1 / (ceiling(l/2) - 1/2) ) * log n ). In particular chi-2(G) and chi-3(G) are in O(n^(1/3) * log n). It is known that this value is also in Omega(n^(1/3)). Therefore, the result gives a bound tight up to a logarithmic factor for l = 2 and l = 3. Previously best-known upper bound for l = 2 was O(n^(1/2)). The authors do not specify if anything non-trivial was known for the other values of l (thus probably not). Note that 2-vertex rankings are equivalent to restricted star colorings.

The first step of the proof is a reduction of the problem to the bounded degree case where we want to prove an upper bound on chi-l(G) of the form Delta^(f(l)) * polylog n. This is achieved by a simple trick of dividing the vertices into low-degree and high-degree vertices and noting that the high-degree part has to be small by the degeneracy assumption. Now, assuming a result for bounded degree we can apply it to the low-degree part and put unique high colors on the vertices of the high-degree part.

Next, the authors construct a coloring of G that is an l-vertex ranking by a two-step procedure. In the first step, the graph is layered (again by looking at the degrees) and the vertices are colored layer-by-layer (with some simple randomness involved). The obtained coloring is not necessarily an l-vertex ranking (even not whp). Thus, in the next step, the authors identify potential problems (problematic paths and vertices). Then, problematic vertices are recolored using higher colors than ones already used. It turns out that this is possible if and only if chromatic number of a certain auxiliary graph is bounded. And so, the rest of the proof is devoted to proving this statement. In fact, the authors prove that this auxiliary graph is degenerate.

In the last part of the proof, some value must be bounded that is in fact a random variable. The authors find another random variable, which behaves similarly and is simpler to study (using Bernstein concentration inequality). Studying this variable takes place in Section 4.

The paper is ok-written (see below). The proofs seem to be correct, however, sometimes more explanation is needed in my opinion (see below). The result is new and solves (almost [up to a log factor], which in this setting does not seem to be very important) an open problem stated in at least two published papers (the case of l=2). Additionally, the result is generalized to any l, which is also interesting and raises new questions about lower bounds. I recommend the paper for publishing after improving the writing.

Reviewer F:

This paper presents improved bounds for the \ell-vertex ranking number of graphs with bounded degeneracy. An \ell-vertex ranking of a graph is a colouring of the vertices with integer labels such that, for any connected subgraph H with diameter at most \ell, there is a vertex in H whose label is larger than that of every other vertex in H.  The \ell-vertex ranking number of a graph is the minimum number of labels required to achieve such a ranking.

The main results of this paper establishes that, for any fixed d and \ell, every d-degenerate n-vertex graph G satisfies \chi_{\ell-vr}(G)=O(n^{1-2/(\ell+1)}(log n)). This result resolves an open problem posed by Karpas, Neiman, and Smorodinsky (2015) in the case \ell=2. Furthermore, the bounds provided for \ell\in \{2,3\} is asymptotically optimal (up to a log n factor). For \ell>3, the asymptotic optimality of these bounds remains an open question.

So far, only a handful of papers have studied the \ell-vertex ranking number of graphs. While the results in the paper are technically strong, I find the broader significance of \ell vertex ranking numbers somewhat unclear. It would be helpful if the authors could provide additional motivation for the study of these parameters.

I’ll now move on to describe their colouring procedure used to prove their main result. Start with a graph G with bounded degeneracy. Since there aren’t “too many” vertices of high degree, a distinct label can be given to each of them thereby reducing the problem to the bounded degree setting. For the remainder of the vertices, a two-phase colouring procedure is applied. The remaining vertices are first partition into a bounded number of sets that are ordered by increasing density. This is used to give a total ordering from which the vertices may be coloured one-by-one.  For each vertex, a special colour palette is determined which reduces the number of conflicts with the already-coloured vertices. A colour is then assigned to the vertex uniformly at random from the palette. As there may be some conflicts that occur, a second phase is used to recolour the problematic vertices. The argument here is more probabilistic using Bernstein’s Inequality to prove with high probability that each problematic vertex has a bounded number of conflicts charged to it. This allows the problematic vertices to be recoloured so that two problematic vertices that receive the same colour are far apart.

Overall, the paper is well-written, and the flow of the argument is very good. I had no problem in assessing the proof for correctness.

In summary, while the significance of \ell-vertex ranking number isn’t too clear to me, I think this paper is of EJC standard since it has a nice result which solves an open problem from the literature. As such, I recommend it for acceptance.
